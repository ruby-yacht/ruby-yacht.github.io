What is ICCC?

A meeting point for computational and human creativity

The International Conference on Computational Creativity is an international forum for studying how computers can participate in creative activities. It has been operating for nearly 20 years and it brings together computer scientists, artists, designers, cognitive scientists, philosophers, and other researchers to investigate creative processes and build systems that can generate, evaluate, or support ideas, artworks, music, stories, designs, and other forms of expression.

For a general audience

ICCC asks what happens when computers become creative tools, collaborators, or audiences. It considers both the possibilities and limits of machine creativity, as well as the ways intelligent systems can expand how people imagine and make things.

For computer science

The field spans generative systems, machine learning, evolutionary computation, search, knowledge representation, language, vision, robotics, human–computer interaction, and computational models of cognition. Researchers study novelty, value, evaluation, autonomy, explanation, and co-creativity.

For the arts

ICCC provides space to examine algorithms, robots, interactive systems, and data as artistic materials. It also raises questions about authorship, intention, agency, originality, taste, cultural meaning, and making work with machines.

Artistic practice

A broad ecology of artists and creative systems

The artist collection shows how computational creativity extends across many forms and working methods. It includes artists such as Anna Ridler, Jake Elwes, Mary Flanagan, Sougwen Chung, Stephanie Dinkins, Mario Klingemann, Memo Akten, Zach Blas, and others working with generative images, artificial intelligence, robotics, data, software, games, performance, and critical media practice.

It also includes collaborative and exhibition-based projects such as the ICCC 2021 virtual art exhibition, the AlphaGarden project, and Arido Taurajo, an operatic machinima developed through human–AI collaboration. Together, these examples point to a field that is not limited to autonomous generation: computational creativity also includes directing, curating, training, interpreting, staging, and critically engaging with systems.
